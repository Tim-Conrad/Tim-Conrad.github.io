Encrypted patches, unlocked on patch day

Ship the fix early as ciphertext and publish the key at release time. Teams stage it in advance and apply it at the same moment, instead of racing attackers.

The problem

A security fix tells an attacker where the bug was. That was always true. What changed in the last year is the price of turning a fix into an exploit.

Keycloak is a good example. Between November 2025 and November 2026 the project tagged 47 releases, about one every eight days, across four supported lines, and fixed at least 50 CVEs [1]. In August 2026 one of them, CVE-2026-18963, let an unauthenticated attacker take over accounts through the password-reset flow [2]. The fixed builds carry tag dates a week before the CVE was published [1], and the public fix is a small change of 57 added lines [3]. A public proof of concept credits Claude for "research and PoC development assistance" and ships "the annotated patch diff" [4]. A team that runs Keycloak has to patch every week, and every patch is a map for someone else.

The numbers behind that feeling are from 2026:

  • Anthropic gave its Mythos Preview model only public patches. The first working Firefox exploit came less than an hour after the patch; Windows kernel exploits cost about $2,000 each. Its summary: "The binding constraint to N-days is now just a few thousand dollars and API access" [5].
  • Elastic found the bug behind a Windows patch "within minutes" of reading the diff with an LLM; the exploit took hours instead of weeks [6].
  • Google counted 141 exploited vulnerabilities from January to August 2026, more than in all of 2025, and puts the growth in "the rapid weaponization of n-days" [7]. Mandiant's estimate for the mean time-to-exploit in 2025 is minus seven days [8], and Rapid7 measured a median of five days from publication to the CISA list of exploited bugs [9].

Finding a new bug with AI works, but it is a search, and search costs. XBOW spent about $40,000 on API calls for a six-day campaign that produced a full Chrome exploit chain [10]. Anthropic's OpenBSD campaign took about 1,000 runs for under $20,000, and the FFmpeg one about $10,000 [11]. A patch removes the search: it says where to look.

The idea of this post: ship the fix days early, but encrypted. Teams download it, plan the change and stage it. At patch day T, only the key is published, and everyone applies the fix at the same moment. Nobody could diff it before T. The goal is not to win the race against the attackers, but to stop it from being a race.

What games do

Games have done this for years. A pre-load puts the game on your disk before launch, and it opens only at release. Steam, as SteamDB describes it, encrypts each depot with AES-256 and one key that is the same for every user, so mirrors and caches can serve the same bytes, and only owners can ask for the key [13].

GTA Online shows the other side. Rockstar ships the content of later updates early inside earlier ones and switches it on from its servers. The files are encrypted, but the keys are in the game, so dataminers read upcoming cars and missions long before they go live. A server switch decides when something is available. It does not keep it secret while the key sits on the device.

For patches, four rules follow:

  • The key is not on the device before T.
  • The device clock never opens anything. Only the published key does.
  • Everything is signed, and the client checks the ciphertext before T.
  • Decryption at T is cheap. One key, one pass.

A design

The update frameworks TUF and Uptane already solve most of this: an offline root key, roles with narrow rights, thresholds of signatures, and protection against rollback and freeze attacks [14][15]. Uptane even names patch diffing as an attacker goal and can carry wrapped keys for encrypted images. What they lack is a key that is published later. The design adds one role for that.

  1. Pre-load. Days before T, the vendor publishes the encrypted bundle as an ordinary target. The manifest with its hash, size and release time is signed by the targets role under the organisation's root key. Clients download it and check the hash.
  2. Release. At T a separate release role signs a short statement: patch id, hash of the ciphertext, the 32-byte content key. A threshold, say two of three officers, keeps one person from releasing early.
  3. Log. Every statement goes into an append-only transparency log, so a release before T, or a different key for some customers, can be seen [16].
  4. Install. The client checks the statement and the log entry, decrypts with the key and installs.

The statement is small and public, so any mirror, CDN or even DNS can carry it. That makes the release hard to block.

key-release statement, about 104 bytes
patch    8 B   id of the patch, points to its manifest entry
key     32 B   AES-256-GCM content key
sig     64 B   Ed25519 over patch id, ciphertext hash and key

The signature covers the hash of the ciphertext from the manifest, so the key belongs to exactly one bundle. With a threshold, each officer adds a signature of 64 bytes.

Without a key server

The key server is the weak spot. It can go down at the wrong moment, someone can force it to wait, or someone can leak the key early. There is a way to release a key on time without any server of the vendor.

A public service called drand publishes a new signed number every three seconds, and each number has a sequence position. A group of independent organisations runs it together, the League of Entropy. No single member can make the number for a position alone: it takes enough of them together, and they make it only when that moment has come [17].

The trick: you can lock data to a number that does not exist yet. The vendor picks the position that falls on patch day T and locks the patch key to it. Until T nobody, the vendor included, can open the lock. At T drand publishes the number, and that number opens it. The free tool tlock does exactly this [17].

The vendor can use both ways at once: its own key server and the drand lock. Then the patch opens at T even if the vendor's server is down. Two limits remain:

  • The vendor still makes the key before it locks it, so someone inside can still leak it.
  • Once locked, the key cannot be held back. If the fix turns out to be broken, it opens at T anyway.

Check the chosen position before you publish. A wrong one, in the past, opens the patch at once.

Air-gapped sites

Offline networks already carry updates in on removable media. With a pre-load, the large part crosses days before T and can be checked against the organisation's key. At T only the key has to cross: the signed statement, or the 48-byte drand number for T, which the site checks against drand's public key, stored there in advance [17]. Either fits in one QR code, which holds up to 2,953 bytes [18], or can be typed in as hex.

A checked drand number also proves that its moment has passed. A site without a reliable clock learns that it is at least that late.

Open source in private

For an open-source project the hard part comes earlier: the fix has to be written and tested without showing it. Today it often shows. In the Keycloak case the fixed builds were tagged a week before the CVE, the port to the main branch was a public pull request whose title does not mention security, and the proof of concept was written from that diff [3][4].

The tools for private work are thin. On GitHub, maintainers can open a temporary private fork from a security advisory. Only invited collaborators see it, but no CI runs there, branch protection does not apply, and merging and publishing are manual steps [19]. On GitLab a merge request in a public project is public; private work needs a separate private fork [20]. Distributions coordinate by mail: the linux-distros list allows an embargo of at most 14 days [21], and the kernel security team holds a fix back for at most 7 days, 14 in exceptional cases [22]. The Git project comes closest to the idea of this post: it prepares signed tags in a private repository, sends a bundle to the distributions less than a week ahead, and pushes in public on release day [23]. That bundle is plaintext and goes to a few.

To make this normal, a forge such as GitHub or GitLab would need (Fig. 7):

  • Private branches and pull requests inside the public repository, visible only to maintainers and a security role.
  • Private CI for those branches, with closed logs and no webhooks to outside apps.
  • Release artefacts that are published encrypted and signed, with the key held by the forge.
  • One action that, at T, publishes the key, merges the branch and publishes the advisory.

The embargo lists fit into this as they are: their members get the key early and test, everyone else gets the ciphertext early. Each early key is a chance to leak, so the list stays short and the embargo stays short.

What it does not fix

  • Diffing after T. At T the patch is public. The scheme moves the start line; it does not stop the race. It helps the hosts that install at once.
  • Zero-days. They are still 62 % of the vulnerabilities Google saw exploited in 2026 [7]. No patch, nothing to encrypt. The scheme is for the part that grows fastest.
  • Slow patching. A host that waits a month after T gains nothing. The scheme helps those who can apply at T.
  • Metadata. File names and sizes still show which component changed. Padding and fixed bundles help, at a cost in bandwidth.
  • Insiders. The key exists before T. Create it late, keep it in an HSM, split it between people.
  • Testing. Companies test patches before they roll them out. Most can stage before T but test only after it. Early keys for a few, as embargo lists do today, bring back the risk of leaks.

Prior art and patents

The idea is not new, and the patents below show it. I found no vendor that does it for security patches today.

Microsoft has patents on it. They are old, but they matter now. This is my reading, not legal advice, and I could not open the claims or the fee records:

  • US 7,876,902, "Distribution of encrypted software update to reduce attack window", priority date 31 August 2006, granted 2011 [24]. A US patent runs 20 years from filing, so it ends around 31 August 2026, later only if the patent office added term for its own delays, and earlier if a maintenance fee was not paid. Its description releases the key once enough machines have the update or after testing, not at a fixed time.
  • US 8,171,560, "Secure content pre-distribution to designated systems", granted 2012 [25]. It is closer to this post: content goes out encrypted and the key follows at a set moment. Filed around 2008, it may run to about 2028.

So the design can be built from public parts, and a fixed release time, a transparency log and a public beacon in place of a vendor key server differ from the first patent's description. Whether that is enough depends on the claims. Anyone who wants to ship this as a product in the US before about 2028 should have a patent attorney read both patents first.

My conclusion: the tool is cheap for an update system that already signs its metadata. It does not stop patch diffing, and with LLMs diffing is now cheap. It lets every team have the fix ready when it becomes public, and that turns patch day from a race into a date.
