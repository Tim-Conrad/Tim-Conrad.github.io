Encrypted patches, unlocked on patch day

Ship the fix early as ciphertext and publish the key at release time. Teams stage it in advance and apply it at the same moment, instead of racing attackers.

The problem

A security patch tells an attacker where the bug was. Compare the old and the new binary, and the fix points at the flaw. In 2008 Brumley and others built working exploits from five Windows patches in seconds to minutes, fully automated [1]. Mandiant measured an average time-to-exploit of 5 days in 2023, down from 63 days in 2018 and 2019 [2].

Most updates also roll out over time. The first host that downloads the patch starts the attacker's clock, while most hosts still wait. The authors of the 2008 paper called this out: staggered rollout can hurt overall security [1].

The idea of this post: ship the fix days early, but encrypted. Teams download it, plan the change and stage it. At patch day T, only the key is published, and everyone applies the fix at the same moment. Nobody could diff it before T. The goal is not to win the race against the attackers, but to stop it from being a race.

What games do

Games have done this for years. A pre-load puts the game on your disk before launch, and it opens only at release.

GTA V shows both sides. In August 2013 the European PlayStation Store let pre-order customers download the game weeks early. People mined the files and story details leaked. Sony apologised and moved the pre-load to four days before launch [3]. Reports disagree on whether those files were encrypted at all. For the PC version in April 2015, the pre-load arrived a week early and stayed encrypted until launch; at launch many players then waited at "unpacking" while the game decrypted [4].

GTA Online does something else. Content for later updates ships early inside earlier updates, and Rockstar switches it on from the server ("tunables"). The archives are encrypted, but the keys are in the game itself, so dataminers list unreleased cars and missions weeks ahead [5]. A server switch decides when content is available. It does not keep the content secret while the key sits on the device.

Steam is closer to what a patch needs. As SteamDB describes it, each depot is encrypted with AES-256 and one key that is the same for every user, so mirrors and caches can serve the same bytes, and only owners can ask for the key [6]. A pre-loaded game stays shut until the publisher releases it. Consoles had the opposite failure: in 2020 players started Cyberpunk 2077 early on Xbox by setting the console to a New Zealand time zone [7].

For patches, four rules follow:

  • The key is not on the device before T.
  • The device clock never opens anything. Only the published key does.
  • Everything is signed, and the client checks the ciphertext before T.
  • Decryption at T is cheap. One key, one pass.

A design

The update frameworks TUF and Uptane already solve most of this: an offline root key, roles with narrow rights, thresholds of signatures, and protection against rollback and freeze attacks [8][9]. Uptane even names patch diffing as an attacker goal and can carry wrapped keys for encrypted images. What they lack is a key that is published later. The design adds one role for that.

  1. Pre-load. Days before T, the vendor publishes the encrypted bundle as an ordinary target. The manifest with its hash, size and release time is signed by the targets role under the organisation's root key. Clients download it and check the hash.
  2. Release. At T a separate release role signs a short statement: patch id, hash of the ciphertext, the 32-byte content key. A threshold, say two of three officers, keeps one person from releasing early.
  3. Log. Every statement goes into an append-only transparency log, so a release before T, or a different key for some customers, can be seen [10].
  4. Install. The client checks the statement and the log entry, decrypts with the key and installs.

The statement is small and public, so any mirror, CDN or even DNS can carry it. That makes the release hard to block.

key-release statement, about 104 bytes
patch    8 B   id of the patch, points to its manifest entry
key     32 B   AES-256-GCM content key
sig     64 B   Ed25519 over patch id, ciphertext hash and key

The signature covers the hash of the ciphertext from the manifest, so the key belongs to exactly one bundle. With a threshold, each officer adds a signature of 64 bytes.

Without a key server

The key server is a single point that can fail, be forced to wait, or leak. Timelock encryption removes it. With tlock, data is encrypted to a future round of the drand beacon, run by the League of Entropy. At that round the network publishes a threshold BLS signature, and that signature is the decryption key [11]. On drand's quicknet a round is 3 seconds and a signature is 48 bytes.

The vendor can wrap the content key twice: once for its own key server, once to the drand round at T. Then the patch opens at T even if the vendor's server is down. Two limits: the vendor still creates the key, so an insider can still leak it; and a timelocked key cannot be pulled back if the fix turns out to be broken. Kudelski Security used tlock for a vulnerability report in 2023; a date bug encrypted it to the first round, so it could be read at once [12]. Test the round number.

Air-gapped sites

Offline networks already carry updates in on removable media. With a pre-load, the large part crosses days before T and can be checked against the organisation's key. At T only the key has to cross: the signed statement, or the 48-byte drand signature, which the site checks against a pinned group key [11]. Either fits in one QR code, which holds up to 2,953 bytes [13], or can be typed in as hex.

A verified drand signature also proves that the time is at least the time of its round. A site without a trusted clock gets a lower bound for free.

Open source in private

For an open-source project the hard part comes earlier: the fix has to be written and tested without showing it. Today a fix often leaks before the release. The Log4Shell fix sat in a public pull request for more than a week before the advisory [18], and researchers built Chrome exploits from public V8 fixes before they reached stable Chrome [19].

The tools for private work are thin. On GitHub, maintainers can open a temporary private fork from a security advisory. Only invited collaborators see it, but no CI runs there, branch protection does not apply, and merging and publishing are manual steps [20]. On GitLab a merge request in a public project is public; private work needs a separate private fork [21]. Distributions coordinate by mail: the linux-distros list allows an embargo of at most 14 days [22], and the kernel security team holds a fix back for at most 7 days, 14 in exceptional cases [23]. The Git project comes closest to the idea of this post: it prepares signed tags in a private repository, sends a bundle to the distributions less than a week ahead, and pushes in public on release day [24]. That bundle is plaintext and goes to a few.

To make this normal, a forge such as GitHub or GitLab would need:

  • Private branches and pull requests inside the public repository, visible only to maintainers and a security role.
  • Private CI for those branches, with closed logs and no webhooks to outside apps.
  • Release artefacts that are published encrypted and signed, with the key held by the forge.
  • One action that, at T, publishes the key, merges the branch and publishes the advisory.

The embargo lists fit into this as they are: their members get the key early and test, everyone else gets the ciphertext early. Each early key is a chance to leak, as the 2012 leak from Microsoft's partner programme showed [15], so the list stays short and the embargo stays short.

What it does not fix

  • Diffing after T. At T the patch is public. The scheme moves the start line; it does not stop the race. It helps the hosts that install at once.
  • Zero-days. Mandiant counted 70 % of exploited vulnerabilities in 2023 as zero-days [2]. No patch, nothing to encrypt.
  • Slow patching. Organisations take weeks to install fixes [14]. A host that waits a month gains nothing.
  • Metadata. File names and sizes still show which component changed. Padding and fixed bundles help, at a cost in bandwidth.
  • Insiders. The key exists before T. Create it late, keep it in an HSM, split it between people.
  • Testing. Companies test patches before they roll them out. Most can stage before T but test only after it. Early keys for a few, as embargo lists do today, bring back the risk of leaks [15].

Prior art and patents

The idea is not new. The 2008 paper on patch-based exploits reportedly proposed encrypted patches with a key released later [16]. Steam pre-loaded Half-Life 2 in 2004, and time-lock puzzles go back to 1996. I found no vendor that does this for security patches today.

Microsoft has patents on it. This is my reading, not legal advice, and I could not open the claims or the fee records:

  • US 7,876,902, "Distribution of encrypted software update to reduce attack window", priority date 31 August 2006, granted 2011 [17]. A US patent runs 20 years from filing, so it ends around 31 August 2026, later only if the patent office added term for its own delays, and earlier if a maintenance fee was not paid. Its description releases the key once enough machines have the update or after testing, not at a fixed time.
  • US 8,171,560, "Secure content pre-distribution to designated systems", granted 2012 [25]. It is closer to this post: content goes out encrypted and the key follows at a set moment. Filed around 2008, it may run to about 2028.

So the design can be built from public parts, and a fixed release time, a transparency log and a public beacon in place of a vendor key server differ from the first patent's description. Whether that is enough depends on the claims. Anyone who wants to ship this as a product in the US before about 2028 should have a patent attorney read both patents first.

My conclusion: the tool is cheap for an update system that already signs its metadata. It does not stop patch diffing, but it lets every team have the fix ready when it becomes public, and that turns patch day from a race into a date.
