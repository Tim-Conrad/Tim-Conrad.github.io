A humans.md for working with models

My repositories tell the agent how to work with me. Nothing tells me how to work with the agent. A first version of that file, and what I found when I looked for one.

The missing file

Every repository of mine has a CLAUDE.md. It tells the agent how to build the project, what to leave alone and how I want my commits. When the agent gets something wrong, I add a line.

There is no such file for me. When a new model comes out, I switch, try a few tasks and go by feel. Sometimes I start a task without any CLAUDE.md, to see whether the new model does better without my old rules. Mostly I just notice things.

That is a strange split. The model gets a written manual and a changelog. The person who drives it gets a hunch. This thought is about the other file: a humans.md, with rules for me, written down and changed when the model changes.

The name is taken

I was not the first to think of it. I found three projects that use the name, and they mean three different things.

So "humans.md" is not my term, and it is not a standard either. Two of the three are files for the agent, about how it should treat me. Mine is the other way round: a file for me, about how I should work with it.

Models change under me

The vendors already write a kind of humans.md for every model. They call it a prompting guide, and the useful part is the section on what changed. Anthropic's guide puts the differences between models first [4]. Three of its examples:

  • Newer models listen more closely to the instructions. Capitals like "CRITICAL: You MUST", which once helped a model use a tool at all, now make it use the tool too often. The guide says to soften them.
  • Claude Opus 5 checks its own work. Old instructions to verify every step make it check again and again. The guide says to remove them.
  • Newer models take the words literally. "Can you suggest some changes?" can get suggestions and no changes.

Other vendors say the same. OpenAI's guide for GPT-5.5 says to treat it as a new model family and to start from a fresh baseline, instead of carrying over every instruction from older prompts [5]. Google recommends that Gemini 3 keep its default temperature of 1.0, although tuning the temperature was normal with older models [6].

A habit that helped with the last model can hurt with this one. And my trick of starting without a CLAUDE.md turns out to be the fresh baseline that OpenAI recommends. I just did it without knowing why.

Feel is a bad meter

That I go by feel worries me because of one study. In early 2025 METR gave 16 experienced open-source developers real tasks in their own projects, some with AI tools and some without [7].

They expected to be 24% faster. Measured, they took 19% longer. Afterwards they still believed they had been 20% faster.

The number itself has aged. METR now marks the 2025 post as out of date. Its update from February 2026 says developers are likely faster than in early 2025, but calls its own data very weak evidence: many developers no longer wanted to work without AI for the test, and the timing breaks down when one person runs several agents at once [8]. Other studies found gains: 21% faster in a randomised trial at Google, with a wide margin of error [15], and 26% more completed tasks in three field experiments with code completion [16].

What stays is the gap between felt and measured. In November 2026 METR put the speed-up of work at Anthropic at about 2x on well-specified tasks, and treated the staff's own estimate of about 12x as very loose evidence [14]. People misjudge their own time in general: they plan too optimistically, and looking at how long past tasks took is what corrects it [19].

Measure without extra work

I will not log my tasks by hand. I know myself. So the numbers have to come from what is already recorded.

Claude Code keeps a transcript of every session on my machine. Besides the conversation, it records the model of each answer, the time of each step, the tokens and their price, and the lines changed [18]. Git adds the other half: Claude's commits carry the model's name in a trailer, and a revert or a fix two days later says something about the code that came before it.

The last question matters most to me. The sessions where I corrected the model most often are a small, personal eval set. After a month they say more about a model than my impression of it, and after a model switch they show whether my instructions still work.

I wrote this down as a skill for my agent, with a script that reads the transcripts and git and prints a report per month and model. The numbers are signs of activity and friction, not of value, and METR treats counts like these as an upper bound for the real gain [17]. They are good for one thing: comparing my own months and models with each other. One catch: Claude Code deletes transcripts after 30 days unless I raise that limit [18].

Agree first, then build

The best habit I picked up this year comes from Matt Pocock's skills for coding agents [9]. Before any code, /grill-me makes the model ask me questions, round after round, until every branch of the plan is decided. Then a spec, then tickets, then the build test first, then a review. A small change skips the spec and the tickets.

What I like about it: it turns the instructions around. I do not tell the model what to do in one long prompt. The model checks whether I know what I want. That works on the one part of the session that I control, and it works with every model.

Stay able to do it

The second rule that holds for every model is about me, not the model. In a study by Anthropic, 52 developers, most of them junior, learned a new Python library [10]. The group with an AI assistant scored 50% on a quiz afterwards, the group that wrote the code by hand 67%. The AI group was not significantly faster.

How people used the AI mattered more than whether they used it:

Those who asked why scored like the hand coders. Those who handed the work over scored below 40%. The groups have two to seven people each, so this is a hint, not a result. It matches a survey of 319 knowledge workers by Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon: the more people trusted the AI, the less critical thinking they reported, and the more they trusted themselves, the more [11]. Their work moved from doing the task to overseeing it.

The authors warn that a skill used only in the rare hard case decays. That is an old finding. In 1983 Lisanne Bainbridge called it one of the ironies of automation: the machine takes the routine work, and the human keeps the hard cases without the practice they need [12]. Simkute and others found the same ironies in generative AI [13].

So: when something breaks, I look first myself, or I ask for the explanation along with the fix. And I keep some small work without a model.

When a new model ships

Put together, the file has two parts. One part holds for every model. The other is a loop that I run each time a model comes out.

Reviewing my CLAUDE.md

One open question of the first version was whether a skill could read a new model card and propose the changes to my CLAUDE.md files. I built one. It reads my session transcripts to see which models I actually use, reads the current prompting guides for those models, runs a fresh research round on the last three months, checks every instruction file of a project, and proposes changes with a reason for each. It changes nothing without my OK.

I ran it on this site. The research found one consistent message, all of it from Anthropic's own docs and none of it measured: keep the always-loaded file under about 200 lines, leave out what the agent can read from the repository, drop capitals and "IMPORTANT", turn rules that must always hold into hooks or checks, and put the check command first [20][21]. Claude Code even has a built-in audit for instructions written for older models [21].

What changed: the workflow for my review notes moved into a skill, with a hook that counts the open notes when a session starts. A script now checks the page rules in CI, where four sentences in four files used to ask for them. The rules for new pages load only when the agent works on a page. The file layout went, because the agent reads the repository anyway. And the file now starts with how to check the work.

Whether the agent works better with it, I do not know yet. That is what the monthly report is for, and the "two weeks later" column of the log.

Some trends I wanted to check found no evidence either way in the last three months: formal verification such as TLA+ as the agent's check, dropping hand-written tests, and deleting code comments so they cannot mislead the model. They stay on my list as open questions.

The file, version 0

This is the first version. It is short on purpose: a rule I do not follow is worse than no rule.

HUMANS.md
# HUMANS.md How I work with language models. The agent has its CLAUDE.md. This file is for me. Version 0.2, 10 Nov 2026. The thinking behind it: https://t7d.dev/thoughts/humans-md/ ## With every model 1. Agree first. For anything bigger than a small fix, let the model ask me questions until the plan is clear. Keep the words we agreed on.2. Say what I want done. "Change it", not "can you suggest". Give the reason for a rule instead of capital letters.3. Give context as to a new colleague: the goal, the limits, where to look.4. Read what I merge. Tests show that it works. Reading shows that I understand it.5. When something breaks, look first myself, or ask for the explanation with the fix.6. Do some small work without a model, regularly, so I can still do it.7. Measure instead of feel, without extra work: once a month and after each model switch, read the report from the session transcripts and git. Look at the sessions that went worst. ## When a new model ships 1. Read the model card and the vendor's prompting guide. Look for what the model does more, what it does less, and what to remove.2. Do one real task without my CLAUDE.md files. Compare.3. Remove what the new model no longer needs: capitals, old workarounds, extra checks.   Let a review skill and `/doctor prompt-audit` in Claude Code find these, then decide myself.4. Write one entry in the log.5. Two weeks later, read the entry again. Was it right? ## Log | Date | Model | What the guide says changed | What I change | Two weeks later || --- | --- | --- | --- | --- || 10 Nov 2026 | Claude Opus 5.5 | Newer Claude models follow instructions literally, check their own work, and react strongly to words like CRITICAL | To try: remove capitals and "verify each step" lines from my CLAUDE.md files | || 10 Nov 2026 | Claude Opus 5.5, Fable 5.2 | Claude Code docs: CLAUDE.md under 200 lines, no file layout, no emphasis, hooks for hard rules, the check command first | t7d.dev CLAUDE.md from 76 to 33 lines: notes into a skill and a hook, a page check in CI, page rules on demand | |

Open questions

  • Should the agent read part of it too? "Ask me questions before a big change" could live in CLAUDE.md as well.
  • Which of the numbers in the monthly report really track what I care about? I will know after a few months of it.
  • Models now come out every few months. Is one log entry per model too much, or too little?
  • Do formal checks, fewer tests or fewer comments help an agent? I found no evidence yet.
