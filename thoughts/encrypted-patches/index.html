Encrypted patches, unlocked on patch day

Ship the fix early as ciphertext and publish the key at release time. Teams stage it in advance and apply it at the same moment, instead of racing attackers.

SpokenEncrypted patches, unlocked on patch day. A thought by Tim Conrad, read aloud. It takes about twenty minutes, in ten short chapters. Three words carry the idea. Patch day, the moment a fix becomes public, written as T. The encrypted bundle, which arrives early. And the key, which arrives at T. When a figure comes up, the narration describes it, so you can listen without the screen.

The problem

A security fix tells an attacker where the bug was. That was always true: compare the old and the new version, a step called patch diffing, and the change points at the bug. What changed in the last year is the price of turning a fix into an exploit.

Keycloak is a good example. Between November 2025 and November 2026 the project tagged 47 releases, about one every eight days, across four supported lines, and fixed at least 50 CVEs [1]. In August 2026 one of them, CVE-2026-18963, let an unauthenticated attacker take over accounts through the password-reset flow [2]. The fixed builds carry tag dates a week before the CVE was published [1], and the public fix is a small change of 57 added lines [3]. A public proof of concept credits Claude for "research and PoC development assistance" and ships "the annotated patch diff" [4]. A team that runs Keycloak has to patch every week, and every patch is a map for someone else.

A bug that is already fixed and public is called an n-day. One that nobody has fixed yet is a zero-day. The numbers for n-days are from 2026:

  • Anthropic gave its Mythos Preview model only public patches. The first working Firefox exploit came less than an hour after the patch. Windows kernel exploits cost about $2,000 each. Its summary: "The binding constraint to N-days is now just a few thousand dollars and API access" [5].
  • Elastic found the bug behind a Windows patch "within minutes" of reading the diff with an LLM. The exploit took hours instead of weeks [6].
  • Google counted 141 exploited vulnerabilities from January to August 2026, more than in all of 2025, and puts the growth in "the rapid weaponization of n-days" [7].
  • Attacks start early. Mandiant estimates that in 2025 exploitation began on average seven days before the fix was public [8]. Rapid7 measured a median of five days from publication to the CISA list of exploited bugs [9].

SpokenThree numbers to keep from that list: an exploit from a patch in under an hour, for about two thousand dollars, and attacks that begin within days of the fix.

Finding a new bug with AI works, but it is a search, and search costs. XBOW spent about $40,000 on API calls for a six-day campaign that produced a full Chrome exploit chain [10]. Anthropic's OpenBSD campaign took about 1,000 runs for under $20,000, and the FFmpeg one about $10,000 [11]. A patch removes the search: it says where to look.

SpokenPause here for a moment. How many days does your team need from a published fix to production?

The idea

The idea of this post: ship the fix days early, but encrypted. Teams download it, plan the change and stage it. At patch day T, only the key is published, and everyone applies the fix at the same moment. Nobody could diff it before T. The goal is not to win the race against the attackers, but to stop it from being a race.

SpokenHas anyone shipped something locked before? Games do it all the time.

Games already ship this way, and at a scale that no patch reaches. A pre-load puts a new game on your disk days before launch, and it opens only at release. Steam, as SteamDB describes it, encrypts each depot with AES-256 and one key that is the same for every user, so mirrors and caches can serve the same bytes, and only owners can ask for the key [13]. That is the half I want: millions of copies in place before the moment, and one small key at the moment.

GTA Online shows what goes wrong. Rockstar ships the content of later updates early inside earlier ones and switches it on from its servers. The files are encrypted, but the keys are in the game, so dataminers read upcoming cars and missions long before they go live. For a patch, that would undo everything: with the key on the device, anyone can diff the fix before T.

So the lesson from games is four rules for patches:

  • The key is not on the device before T.
  • The device clock never opens anything. Only the published key does.
  • Everything is signed, and the client checks the ciphertext before T.
  • Decryption at T is cheap. One key, one pass.

SpokenThose four rules are easy to write down. The next question is how to build them on update systems that already exist.

A design

A signature on the patch alone is not enough. If the one signing key leaks, the thief can sign malware. A mirror can also cheat without any key. It can serve an older version with a known bug, a rollback attack. Or it can keep serving yesterday's list of updates, so the client never learns that a fix exists, a freeze attack.

The Update Framework, TUF, was designed against exactly these attacks [14]. It splits trust into roles, and each role has its own keys:

  • Root names the keys of all other roles. Its keys stay offline and are needed only to change those keys.
  • Targets signs the list of files, with the hash and size of each.
  • Snapshot signs which version of each list is current, so a mirror cannot mix old and new lists.
  • Timestamp signs a short note that says what is newest. The note expires quickly, so a freeze shows.

A role can also require a threshold: k of its n keys must sign, so one stolen key is not enough. Uptane is TUF adapted for cars [15]. It adds a second repository, the director, which tells each vehicle which images to install, and the director can encrypt images for each control unit. The Uptane standard even names patch diffing as an attacker goal.

What neither has is a key that is published later. The design adds one role for that, the release role, next to targets:

  1. Pre-load. Days before T, the vendor publishes the encrypted bundle as an ordinary target. The manifest with its hash, size and release time is signed by the targets role under the organisation's root key. Clients download it and check the hash.
  2. Release. At T the release role signs a short statement: patch id, hash of the ciphertext, the 32-byte content key. A threshold keeps one person from releasing early: say three officers each hold a key, and any two of them must sign.
  3. Log. Every statement goes into a transparency log. That is a public list that only grows: entries can be added but not changed or removed, and anyone can check that an entry is in it. Certificate Transparency uses such logs for TLS certificates [16]. Anyone who watches the log sees a release before T, or a different key for some customers.
  4. Install. The client checks the statement and the log entry, decrypts with the key and installs.

The statement is small and public, so any mirror, CDN or even DNS can carry it. That makes the release hard to block.

key-release statement, about 104 bytes
patch    8 B   id of the patch, points to its manifest entry
key     32 B   AES-256-GCM content key
sig     64 B   Ed25519 over patch id, ciphertext hash and key

The signature covers the hash of the ciphertext from the manifest, so the key belongs to exactly one bundle. With a threshold, each officer adds a signature of 64 bytes.

Without a key server

The key server is the weak spot. It can go down at the wrong moment, someone can force it to wait, or someone can leak the key early. There is a way to release a key on time without any server of the vendor.

drand is a public randomness beacon [17]. Every three seconds it publishes a new round: a round number and a value. The value is a signature over the round number, made with a key that nobody holds as a whole. When the network was set up, its members, a group of independent organisations called the League of Entropy, split that key into shares, and each member keeps one share.

For each round, every member signs the round number with its share when that round's time has come. Enough of these partial signatures together give the full signature, and anyone can check it against one public key that never changes. Cryptographers call this a threshold signature. So nobody can compute the value of a future round, not even a member alone. It takes enough members, and honest members sign a round only at its time.

The second part is a kind of encryption where the key can be a name. In identity-based encryption, anyone can encrypt to a name, an email address for example, with one public master key. The private key for that name is made later, by whoever holds the master secret. In drand, the name is a round number, the master key is drand's public key, and the signature for a round is the private key for it [17]. The master secret is the split key of the League, so the private key for a round appears only when the members sign that round.

So the vendor picks the round that falls on patch day T and encrypts the patch key to that round. Until T nobody, the vendor included, can decrypt it. At T drand publishes the signature for that round, and the signature opens it. The free tool tlock, from the drand team, does exactly this [17].

The vendor can use both ways at once: its own key server and the drand lock. Then the patch opens at T even if the vendor's server is down. Two limits remain:

  • The vendor still makes the key before it locks it, so someone inside can still leak it.
  • Once locked, the key cannot be held back. If the fix turns out to be broken, it opens at T anyway.

Check the chosen position before you publish. A wrong one, in the past, opens the patch at once.

Air-gapped sites

Offline networks already carry updates in on removable media. With a pre-load, the large part crosses days before T and can be checked against the organisation's key. At T only the key has to cross: the signed statement, or the 48-byte drand number for T, which the site checks against drand's public key, stored there in advance [17]. Either fits in one QR code, which holds up to 2,953 bytes [18], or can be typed in as hex.

A checked drand number also proves that its moment has passed. A site without a reliable clock learns that it is at least that late.

Open source in private

For an open-source project the hard part comes earlier: the fix has to be written and tested without showing it. Today it often shows. In the Keycloak case the fixed builds were tagged a week before the CVE, the port to the main branch was a public pull request whose title does not mention security, and the proof of concept was written from that diff [3][4].

The tools for private work are thin. On GitHub, maintainers can open a temporary private fork from a security advisory. Only invited collaborators see it, but no CI runs there, branch protection does not apply, and merging and publishing are manual steps [19]. On GitLab a merge request in a public project is public; private work needs a separate private fork [20]. Distributions coordinate by mail, under an embargo: a fixed time in which everyone who knows about the bug keeps quiet. The linux-distros list allows an embargo of at most 14 days [21], and the kernel security team holds a fix back for at most 7 days, 14 in exceptional cases [22]. The Git project comes closest to the idea of this post: it prepares signed tags in a private repository, sends a bundle to the distributions less than a week ahead, and pushes in public on release day [23]. That bundle is plaintext and goes to a few.

To make this normal, a forge such as GitHub or GitLab would need (Fig. 7):

  • Private branches and pull requests inside the public repository, visible only to maintainers and a security role.
  • Private CI for those branches, with closed logs and no webhooks to outside apps.
  • Release artefacts that are published encrypted and signed, with the key held by the forge.
  • One action that, at T, publishes the key, merges the branch and publishes the advisory.

The embargo lists fit into this as they are: their members get the key early and test, everyone else gets the ciphertext early. Each early key is a chance to leak, so the list stays short and the embargo stays short.

SpokenThat is the design. Here is what it does not solve.

What it does not fix

  • Diffing after T. At T the patch is public. The scheme moves the start line; it does not stop the race. It helps the hosts that install at once.
  • Zero-days. They are still 62 % of the vulnerabilities Google saw exploited in 2026 [7]. No patch, nothing to encrypt. The scheme is for the part that grows fastest.
  • Slow patching. A host that waits a month after T gains nothing. The scheme helps those who can apply at T.
  • Metadata. File names and sizes still show which component changed. Padding and fixed bundles help, at a cost in bandwidth.
  • Insiders. The key exists before T. Create it late, keep it in an HSM, split it between people.
  • Testing. Companies test patches before they roll them out. Most can stage before T but test only after it. Early keys for a few, as embargo lists do today, bring back the risk of leaks.

Prior art

The idea is not new. Microsoft patented an encrypted update with a key that follows later, first in 2006 [24] and in a version with a set release moment around 2008 [25]. I found no vendor that does it for security patches today. By my reading, which is not legal advice, the first patent ends around August 2026 and the second may run to about 2028. Anyone who wants to ship this as a product in the US before then should have a patent attorney read both.

What I take from it

The tool is cheap for an update system that already signs its metadata. It does not stop patch diffing, and with LLMs diffing is now cheap. It lets every team have the fix ready when it becomes public, and that turns patch day from a race into a date.

SpokenTo sum up. Patches are now cheap to turn into exploits. Ship the fix early but encrypted, publish only the key at T, and every team gets the same start line. The parts exist: TUF or Uptane for the signatures, a transparency log for the release, and drand with tlock for a release without a key server. The sources are on the page.
